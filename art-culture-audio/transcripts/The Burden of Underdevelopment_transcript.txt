Until the 1970s, the terms poor countries and underdeveloped countries were virtually interchangeable. But the emergence of oil power has shattered this easy equation. Virtually all third world countries are still technically underdeveloped, but only some of them are now poor. South Yemen and Tanzania are still good illustrations of the old equation. But in the 1970s, it's become difficult to think of Saudi Arabia, for example, as a poor nation. On the contrary, this is one of the best endowed countries in the world, in oil wealth and dollar reserves while being at the same time, one of the least developed. What is true of Saudi Arabia as a country is substantially true of Africa as a continent. In terms of resources, Africa is one of the best endowed regions of the world. But it is still the least developed of the inhabited continents. This is the pathology of technical backwardness. A related paradox is that, ahead of each group's population, the richest inhabitants of Africa are non-Africans. The poorest in Parcabitat terms are indigenous Africans themselves. This is one reason why the highest standards of living are among white people in southern Africa. This is the pathology of maldistribution. The third interrelated paradox is that while the continent as a whole is rich in resources, it is so fragmented that it includes the majority of the poorest nations of the world. This is the pathology of a fragmented economy. Let us look at this paradox of a well-indowed continent inhabited by under privileged natives. Estimates of Africa's resources are on the whole tentative, but it's already fair to say that Africa has 96% of the non-communist world's diamonds. 60% of its gold, 42% of its cobalt, 34% of its boxide, and 28% of its uranium. The West's interest in Africa's oil has also significantly increased. Partly in proportion to the political uncertainties surrounding the Middle Eastern suppliers. Had Nigeria joined the Arab oil embargo of the United States in 1973, the consequences for America would have been severe. Then there is Africa's agricultural potential. The Sudan, Africa's largest country in square miles may indeed develop into a major bread basket for parts of Africa and the Middle East before the end of this century. Then there are Africa's water resources with some of the greatest rivers of the world. The potential for building dams and generating hydroelectric power have only just begun to be exploited. In short, the continent itself seems to be well-indowed with resources, but a disproportionate number of people in the population of the continent are undernourished and underprivileged, a situation of anomalous underdevelopment. A substantial part of the explanation lies in the nature of Africa's economic links with the Western world across time. Trade is the oldest of the different areas of economic interaction. After European trade goes back to the slave trade and to the traffic in firearms and beyond. In subsequent years, there was also investment in Africa's mineral and agricultural wealth and sources of power and energy. This grew at a modest pace until the second world war. The war was itself an important divide in Africa's history. It helps to liberate the continent politically, but it also enslaved Africa economically. The political liberation was achieved partly by undermining Western Europe's capacity to hold on to empires. Britain was exhausted and substantially impoverished by the time the war ended. France had been humiliated in defeat at the hands of the Germans. Related to all this was the destruction of the myth of European invincibility in the eyes of the colonized peoples. Suddenly somebody in Bombay noticed that the emperor's new clothes of modern technology were not clothes at all. The British Raj was naked. At a more individual level, the war also cuts the white man down to size in African eyes. The colonized Africans had had their humanity reduced because they were regarded as part devils and part monkeys. They certainly had their adulthood reduced when they were often equated with children. As I was growing up in Bombay in the 1940s, the film sensors declared some films as being not suitable for Africans and children under 16. On the other hand, Europeans had been portrayed as super adults and virtually superhuman. To observe a white man scared to death and a fire was itself a revelation to many Africans, who had previously seen white men only in their arrogant commanding posture as a colonial elite. Another effect of the war was to broaden the general social and political horizons, not only of ex-service men, but of many Africans who had remained behind. For millions of Africans all over the continent, the second world war was an important internationalizing experience. By the end of it many of them were ready to agitate for freedom and independence. The principal consequence of the war was that the pinnacle of world power was no longer in Western Europe but divided between Washington and Moscow. The two superpowers both had a tradition of anti-imperialism in at least some sense, though both are also guilty of other forms of imperialism. What is clear is that the rise of the Soviet Union and the preeminence of the United States after the Second World War created pressures on European powers to make concessions to African nationalists struggling for independence. Paradoxically the economic impact of the war on Africa was enslaving rather than liberating. So what can be done to help Africa get out of this predicament? Can it modernise without westernising? I would define modernisation as change in a direction which is compatible with the present stage of human knowledge and which does justice to the human person as an innovative and social being. If a society does not want to take into account, present their levels of knowledge, science and scholarship, that society is pre-modern. If a society suppresses innovation and insists on doing things according only to tradition, again that society is pre-modern. If a society interprets the concept of man as a social being too narrowly limiting social loyalty to clans and tribes or even just donations and turns its back on the outer world of fellow human beings, that society is still pre-modern. If you define modernity then according to these three basic principles of responsiveness to the highest levels of knowledge, encouragement of innovation and enlargement of social sympathies, they are clearly different roads to modernity. For the third world the best way forward is to adopt those three principles of modernity on the one hand and on the other to pursue strategies of decolonisation and of reducing dependency. It's a dual effort to modernise and decolonise at the same time. In essence it's an effort to decolonise modernity. How can we marry these three principles to strategies of decolonisation and liberation? I think there are a number of possibilities. The first is to some extent already underway the strategy of indigenisation of using local manpower and other local resources as fast possible. Africa must also have greater use of its traditional technology and know-how. The struggle for simpler forms of technology is part of the process of reducing reliance on outsiders for sophisticated know-how. In recent times there has been increased interest in traditional African medicine for example with special reference to some of the effective herbs which were used in therapy. My second way of transcending dependency is what I call the strategy of domestication. This involves making a resource which is foreign more relevant and more appropriate for the African situation. For example Parliament in Nairobi is in conception of foreign institution. It has all the paraphernalia of Parliament at Westminster without the sanctity of tradition and without the stamp of authenticity. Then there is the domestication of the economy. This involves a reduction of its export orientation and an enhancement of the focus on domestic food needs and other local requirements. Sometimes emphasizing labor intensive technology instead of capital intensive is itself a form of domesticating the techniques of production, distribution, communication and exchange. Labor intensive technology is a form of democratizing modernity involving more and more people in the process of modern production and utilizing a major resource of even the poorest of the African countries. The human resource. My third strategy for transcending dependency is diversification. I start from the premise that there are occasions when freedom begins with the multiplication of one's masters. I do not agree therefore with those scholars and analysts who feel the third world should actually disengage from the international capitalist system. It's too late to disengage. The industrialized countries could gradually destroy the world through pollution and other forms of ecological damage or they could impoverish it by an indiscriminate or even reckless depletion of its resources.
